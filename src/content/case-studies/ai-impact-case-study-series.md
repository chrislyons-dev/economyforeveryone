A practical series for **An Economy for Everyone (E4E)**.

These aren't "AI takes" and they're not a tour of every industry AI touches. They're **bounded case studies** - chosen to keep the scope honest while pulling out **lessons that travel** to similar situations.

AI changes the game in two ways. Sometimes it **amplifies an old failure** by making it faster, cheaper, and harder to contest. Sometimes it **unlocks a new operating model** by making coordination, targeting, or content production possible at scales that were previously impractical. In both cases, the right question is not "is AI present?" but "what power does it shift, and who gains from that shift?"

The goal is concrete, repeatable action:

- reduce the monthly squeeze
- keep systems contestable
- prevent quiet capture at the top
- keep real choice (exit) alive

## The common spine

The same pattern shows up across every domain in this series:

AI often does not invent a new incentive problem. It makes an existing one faster, cheaper, and harder to see. When decisions get **fast, cheap, and opaque**, "human review" becomes a rubber stamp. That's how power quietly moves from people to institutions.

Two tests show up everywhere:

### Test 1: Human Command (minimum floor)

If AI affects a life outcome, you get:

- notice
- reason
- appeal
- records
- a human override

### Test 2: Exit / captivity

If you can't leave, you're captured. When exit isn't realistic, governance has to be stronger - not weaker.

## Why these are industry-specific

Specific industries work better than abstract arguments because they:

- make the mechanism concrete (real decisions, real harm, real incentives)
- give you measurable proxies (appeal rates, reversals, time-to-action, gatekeeping)
- keep the research bounded
- let you generalize honestly ("this pattern shows up here; here's why it shows up elsewhere too")

Each case study aims to give you:

- a clear mechanism ("what's happening and why")
- a minimum floor (what has to be true for real contestability)
- levers (what to change, who can change it, how to know if it worked)

This also helps separate two different kinds of AI risk:

- old patterns getting scaled up to machine speed
- genuinely new capabilities that create new failure paths

You need both categories in view. If you miss the first, you treat today's harms like they came out of nowhere. If you miss the second, you assume the future will just be the past turned up louder.

## How to read / use this series

1. Read the opening scene at the top of each study - the lived reality.
2. Find the mechanism - the pattern that keeps repeating.
3. Run the Human Command test and the Exit test.
4. Pick one lever from the levers section:
   - something a normal person can do
   - something an institution, employer, or procurement office can do

Each case study draws from a shared library of **Mechanisms** and **Mechanism Modules**. Not every case uses every mechanism the same way, but the vocabulary stays consistent across the series - so when you read the second study, the third goes faster.

## What to watch for as you read

The thing that keeps showing up: **AI doesn't automatically point in a good direction.**

A lot of the underlying behavior is not new. What is new is that AI can industrialize it.

In every case, the same capability can go two ways:

- reduce friction, widen access, improve accountability, make the system fairer and easier to live with
- or speed up extraction, hide responsibility, weaken contestability, make things more stressful, more opaque, and harder to escape

That's not "AI is neutral." It's that **the first institutional instinct matters a lot.**

The default deployment path usually leans toward short-term wins:

- faster throughput
- lower labor cost
- tighter control
- more hidden pricing power
- more nominal review with less real accountability

Those wins are often real in the short run - and still damaging in the long run if they hollow out learning, weaken appeals, trap people in captive systems, or let gains flow upward while the people affected see nothing.

So while reading each case, keep these questions in mind:

1. **Is this mostly an old problem running at new scale, or a genuinely new capacity?**
   Is AI just making an existing failure cheaper and faster, or is it creating a new operating model that changes the structure of the market?

2. **What is this system actually optimizing for?**
   Cost, speed, control, margin? Or fairness, reliability, and better outcomes for the people using it?

3. **How could the same capability be used better?**
   Not in theory - through what specific guardrails, incentives, disclosures, or procurement terms?

4. **Is this a short-term win that creates a long-term institutional loss?**
   Faster denials, thinner apprenticeship, weaker trust, more downstream stress for households and communities?

That's the core reason this series exists. The goal isn't to describe AI harms after they've already scaled. It's to catch where the technology _could_ make systems fairer and more accountable - and where current incentives push it the other way instead.

Across the studies, keep this in mind: the most immediate AI disruption is often not that it invented a new kind of harm. It is that it made an old one cheap enough to run continuously. Then, on top of that, new capabilities start to appear. Good governance has to be ready for both.

## The case studies

### [1) Claims and eligibility](/case-studies/claims-eligibility/)

**What it studies:** automated or semi-automated decisions about eligibility, coverage, payment, and access - insurance, benefits, healthcare admin, financial access.

**Why it's useful:** this is where "rubber-stamp review" shows up in actual data:

- low appeal rates
- high overturn rates when people do appeal
- vague reason codes
- asymmetric logs

**Generalizes to:** government benefits, disability decisions, fraud flags, credit/underwriting, provider payment - any system where a "no" is cheap and contesting it is costly.

**Primary levers:** reason specificity in denial notices, appeal timelines with teeth, audit logs, independent review, procurement requirements, portability and exit constraints.

### [2) Personalized pricing and steering](/case-studies/personalized-pricing-steering/)

**What it studies:** individualized offers, routing, eligibility nudges, and price discrimination in captive or semi-captive markets.

**Why it's useful:** it shows the shift from "pricing as information" to "pricing as extraction" when:

- comparison shopping is hard
- the offer is personalized
- the logic is hidden
- switching costs are high

**Generalizes to:** housing, insurance premiums, healthcare routing, lending, utility add-ons, consumer marketplaces, and any "surge" or segmentation system.

**Primary levers:** all-in pricing disclosure, non-discrimination audits (including proxy tests), contestable adverse actions, portability and switching tools, limits on exploitative personalization in captive markets.

### [3) Surveillance and coercion](/case-studies/surveillance-coercion/)

**What it studies:** cheap sensing, cheap scoring, and cheap enforcement - especially where consequences arrive before any adjudication.

**Why it's useful:** it draws a clear line between two failure modes:

- accuracy failures (wrong outputs)
- rights failures (even accurate outputs with no contestability, no exit, no audit)

Scale without accountability produces coercion. That's the test.

**Generalizes to:** workplace monitoring, gig deactivation, fraud detection, benefits enforcement, immigration enforcement, risk scoring in many forms, private-sector data broker ecosystems.

**Primary levers:** independent corroboration before enforcement, audit logs, query constraints, appeal rights with timelines, prohibited-use categories in contracts, kill switches, transparency reporting, limits on warrantless purchase of sensitive data.

### [4) Content flood and gate shift](/case-studies/content-flood-gate-shift/)

**What it studies:** near-zero-cost content production and the resulting shift of power to distribution, ranking, and trust layers.

**Why it's useful:** it surfaces a general rule - when production gets cheap, **attention and trust** become scarce, and power moves to whoever controls ranking, verification, and gatekeeping.

**Generalizes to:** app stores, marketplaces, job boards, social platforms, academic publishing, reviews/ratings - any domain where discovery is the choke point.

**Primary levers:** provenance and labeling, contestable ranking and takedown, anti-spam friction, preservation of credentials through pipelines, transparency requirements, competition constraints on gatekeepers.

### [5) IT ladder collapse](/case-studies/it-ladder-collapse/)

**What it studies:** what happens to skill formation when "learning work" gets automated away and entry-level ladders shrink.

**Why it's useful:** it captures a second-order effect most people miss - institutions can lose the ability to do the work even if they want to, later. When apprenticeship collapses, oversight becomes fake.

**Generalizes to:** claims adjusters, caseworkers, compliance analysts, junior reporters and editors, entry-level accountants, paralegals - any profession with a training pipeline.

**Primary levers:** protected learning-work quotas, apprenticeship funding, review rotations, "manual flight checks," measurable hiring and mentorship ratios, procurement rules requiring retained human capability.

## What "good" looks like

What good looks like is not "AI everywhere" or "AI nowhere." It is AI used in ways that lower friction for normal people, preserve real choice, keep human command meaningful, and make sure gains are actually shared instead of quietly captured.

A system that uses AI well tends to have:

- visible and actionable reasons at decision time
- cheap, real appeals - not symbolic ones
- logs that auditors can inspect and, when appropriate, the affected person can access
- human overrides that come with actual authority and time to use it
- clear exit paths - or stronger governance where exit is impossible
- evidence that gains are shared (lower prices, better access, less admin drag) rather than captured as margin
