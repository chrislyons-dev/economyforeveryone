# The Capture Rule

**The greater the rewards for control, the stronger the safeguards must be.**

Some institutions can run on culture. A neighborhood group with no budget and no authority can rely on relationships, shared norms, and informal accountability. There's very little worth capturing. If a bad actor takes over, the damage is limited.

Government is different. Government controls money, laws, appointments, contracts, and coercive authority. Even with private money removed entirely, people still compete for status, influence, ideological control, and institutional power. The rewards for control are enormous, and culture alone can't hold them.

**Trust matters most where power is greatest. It's also where trust alone is least sufficient.**

## The institutional spectrum

| Institution            | Capture reward | Primary defense                                      |
| ---------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Neighborhood group     | Low            | Culture and relationships                            |
| Volunteer organization | Low - moderate | Culture + transparency                               |
| School district        | Moderate       | Elections + transparency                             |
| City government        | High           | Oversight + audits + elections                       |
| Federal government     | Very high      | Constitutional guardrails + independent institutions |

The spectrum is the point: match the safeguard to the reward. Under-guard a high-reward institution and it gets captured. Over-guard a low-reward one and you bury volunteers in compliance theater.

## Design for ordinary human behavior

People in institutions respond to the same pressures everyone else does. The goal is institutions built for ordinary human behavior, not ones that bet on extraordinary virtue.

Where capture rewards are high, that means:

- transparency by default
- independent oversight, so no one grades their own work
- checks that keep any single actor from controlling too much
- simple, enforceable rules
- distributed power

## The price of independence

Independent institutions are slow and frustrating, and they occasionally block things that seem obviously good. They are supposed to. An institution that only frustrates bad actors and never good ones isn't independent; it's aligned with whoever holds power today.

The same structure that blocks a corrupt government from suppressing evidence will sometimes block a reform government from acting on urgency. That's the price of independence. The alternative is a system where whoever wins the next election inherits powers that can be exploited by the next person willing to use them.

## How to test a safeguard

Run any claimed reform to an oversight institution through the [Guardrail Stress Test](/levers/guardrail-stress-test/). The two most portable criteria:

- **The opponent test:** would this design still be acceptable if political opponents controlled it?
- **Tighter constraints:** does it bind the next government too, including the one proposing it?

## Applies beyond government

The rule scales down. Inside companies, churches, nonprofits, unions, and schools: where the rewards for control are high (budget authority, hiring power, information control), culture isn't enough. Transparent governance, rotating leadership, independent review, and simple enforceable rules are how trust survives contact with ordinary human incentives.
